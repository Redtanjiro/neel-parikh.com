What is CSEDSCSEDS?

One name, three businesses, and a website that had to admit it.

The problem of one name over three businesses.

What does a thirty-year-old firm say first, when it has quietly become three companies?

CSEDS Australia has been engineering since 1991. It is a consulting practice, a supplier of construction products — Barchip, TrueGrid, TripStop, Prestige Modular — and the Australian channel for a shelf of international engineering software. One name over all three, and one website trying to carry them.

Who is the consulting for?

Councils and developers who need an engineer, not a catalogue. It sells on judgement, and thirty years of it.

Who are the products for?

Specifiers and builders. They want compliance ratings, load data and a datasheet — fast.

Who is the software for?

Practising engineers buying licences. They want to know the tool is supported locally by someone who uses it.

“Every visitor reads something written for somebody else.”

What one voice for all three would have done

How did CSEDS solve this?

One shell. Change exactly one variable.

Every template shares the same header, type, labels, feature lists and footer. The only thing that moves between them is the accent.

Steel blue for products.A visitor two scrolls into a product page knows where they are without being told.
Oxide red for software.The subject changes to ATENA, CivilGeo and DeepExcavation. The grid, the type and the footer don’t.
The stamp never moves.The nav, the // SINCE 1991 // stamp and the quote button are identical on every page, so nobody feels they have left the company.

What I did

Product and web design, solo, working directly with the founder over a few weeks in 2025. A landing page, a product template, a software template and the mobile pass — designed in Figma and built by me, through to the live site at csedsaust.com.au.

The one value that moved

Can I use it on site?

Most of the traffic is a specifier on a site with one hand free.

The feature lists stack, the spec table becomes a single column, and the quote button stays reachable — the hierarchy survives the narrow measure because it was never carried by the layout.

The same page, one hand free

That’s the visitor’s side.

But the site has a second reader — whoever makes the fifth product page.

A firm that has spent thirty years adding to its catalogue will keep adding to it. That was the actual brief underneath the stated one. The test of this work is not how the launch looked — it is whether the next page can be made by someone who was never in the room.

There were two other ways to do this. Both were real options, and both fail for a reason worth drawing.

Flatten them into one

  1. One voice
  2. One template
  3. Everyone reads somebody else’s page

Rejected. A council specifier and a licence buyer get the same page.

Split them into three

  1. Three sites
  2. Three identities
  3. A thirty-year firm looks like three small ones

Rejected. The age and the range stop counting for anything.

One shell, one variable

  1. One shell
  2. One variable moves
  3. Each line reads as its own

Taken. Three businesses, and still visibly one company.

What the visitor ends up with

The same proof block, in red

The software page’s proof section and footer are the landing page’s, with one value changed. Nobody had to design them twice.

What went over

It is live at csedsaust.com.au, and the next product does not need me.

I designed and built it. That is the reason the template argument is more than a diagram: the thing that had to survive contact with a real build was a rule I had to implement myself, so there was nowhere for a nice idea to hide.

What I’d do differently

No before-and-after traffic, no enquiry numbers and no user testing. I don’t have a capture of the old site either, so the “before” here is described rather than shown. The strongest evidence I can offer is that the site is live and you can go and use it.

The part I’d push harder is the consulting line. It got the shell and the grid, but not the same argument the other two got — it sells on judgement, and judgement is the hardest thing to put in a feature list.
