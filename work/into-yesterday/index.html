Summary

A short film about going back to a memory on purpose, and finding it has a floor plan.

I made all of it: the concept, the boards, the model, the lighting, the render and the score. It started as clay — nine small tiles of colour and texture made by hand — and ended as a lit maze standing in dark grass beside black water.

The idea

Memory is navigable. That's the problem with it.

You don't drift into the past — you go, deliberately, and you know the turns. The maze is warm and lit from inside because that's how the memory advertises itself. The gems in the water are the parts you can see and can't reach: bright, close, on the other side of a wall you built.

Everything in the film follows from that one image. The walls glow. The grass outside doesn't.

Where it started

Before any 3D, I made the palette by hand — clay pressed into small square tiles, photographed outside on grass and stone. Fast, physical, and impossible to fake in a colour picker. The film's whole range of amber, oxide red and bruised blue came out of these.

Reference

The look sits between two things that shouldn't go together: the soft, sunlit nostalgia of a kid on a hill, and the hard geometry of a floor plan seen from above. The moodboard holds both so the film could keep checking itself against them.

Building it

The maze is one modelled object, lit from within so the walls carry the light rather than receive it. Everything else in frame — the grass, the stone edge, the water, the gems — exists to be the thing the maze is not.

The walk

Four boards, and each one is closer than the last. The camera never cuts away and never turns around — the only direction available is further in. Keep scrolling and you go with it.

What I'd keep

Making the palette out of clay before opening Blender.

It meant every colour decision was already made, by hand, in a medium that pushes back — so the render had something to be faithful to instead of something to invent. I'd do that first on every project now, 3D or not.

What I'd change: the film states its image beautifully and then holds it. It needed one moment where the maze does something the audience doesn't expect — a wall that moves, a light that goes out. The idea was strong enough to survive being tested, and I never tested it.
