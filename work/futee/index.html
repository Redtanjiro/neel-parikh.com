Futee UX design

What is FuteeFutee?

A total solution for a football player’s needs, from finding pickup games to coaches.

The problem of being a solo football player.

How do you get a game when the people organising one have no way of finding you?

Once players leave school or university, the pickup game goes with them. Organising one means a group chat, a pitch nobody has vouched for, and a squad of strangers whose skill level you find out at kickoff.

Where do you play?

A pitch nobody has vouched for. There is no way to judge a venue until you have already paid for it.

Who do you play with?

Whoever answers the group chat. The creator stalls at venue selection; the player stalls waiting to hear back, with no signal that an application went anywhere.

How good will the game be?

Nobody knows until kickoff. Standard is the one thing no organiser can promise and every player wants.

“Finding a good game is a coin flip.”

A regular player, Bangkok — from the questionnaire and calls

How did Futee solve this?

A date, a pitch, three taps.

Every finding was inverted into a feature, one at a time. Three of them carried the product.

Create, then fill.The brief assumed you gather a squad and then book. Research said the opposite, so the game is real at step one and joining is a tap, not a negotiation.
One screen, no navigating.The date strip, the venue and every open game sit together, so a player never has to leave a screen in order to compare.
Yellow means decide.Yellow has exactly one job in the system: it marks the thing you are being asked to decide. Everything else is black, white and the pitch.

What I did

UX research, information architecture, the UI system and the visual identity, over a fourteen-week engagement across 2021–22. A team project, not a solo one — the stakeholder and player calls were run together rather than outsourced. Seventeen screens across web and iOS, handed to an external firm to build.

The life of a game

Every game goes through the same six stages. I mapped how the person running it feels across them, and the mood drops in two places — choosing the venue, and rating afterwards. Both got a feature of their own.

Search

Excited, and already thinking about everyone else’s convenience.

Choose

A bunch of venues and no way to judge them. Hence top-rated, certified pitches.

Make

Public or private, and a match link generated on the spot.

Share

The link goes to the inner circle, or out in public.

Play

Split the teams, then the game itself.

Rate

The chore after the fun. Hence rating built into the flow, not left to memory.

Where the mood dips

Journey map — the game creator

That’s the player’s side.

But every game on Futee has a second person behind it — the one who made it. The two never speak to each other, and every screen has to serve both.

Desk research, a questionnaire, and calls with stakeholders in Thailand and regular players in Bangkok produced two personas. They stall in different places.

The creator — Chatri, 18

A student who organises games to meet new players and get better. He stalls at venue selection, with no way to judge a pitch.

The player — Chai, 47

“I used to play football regularly when I was in school and college… But I still love the game.”

He works full time and has no time to organise. He stalls waiting to hear back, with no signal that his request went anywhere.

The brief assumed you gather a squad and then book something. The research said the opposite. It was the one real argument with the brief, so here it is drawn out, including the version that lost.

What the brief assumed — fill, then create

  1. Find players
  2. Agree a time
  3. Book a pitch
  4. Game exists

Rejected. Nothing exists until everyone agrees, and nobody knows if they’re in.

What the research pointed to — create, then fill

  1. Pick a date and pitch
  2. Game exists
  3. Players join
  4. Squad fills

Taken. The game is real at step two, and joining is a tap, not a negotiation.

Problems, inverted

Every finding became a feature, one at a time. Finding reliable players became the organiser’s rating. Wanting a game soon became open games in your area. Building a circle became direct invites.

Nothing went into the product that couldn’t be traced back to research.

Information architecture

The register flow and the homepage tree sit on one sitemap. Creating a game, finding one, the directory and coaches all hang off the same home, so the creator and the player never end up in different products.

What went over

It went to the stakeholders as a system, not a screen set — two personas, two journeys, a sitemap traceable back to research, and a UI that holds the same rules whether you’re organising a game or joining one. Create-then-fill was adopted without argument, because the journey map made the case before I did.

An external development firm built it after handover. It ran, and it has since been taken down. I don’t have usage figures from the period it was live, and I’d rather say that than imply a launch I can’t evidence.

What I’d do differently

There is no usability testing on this page, and no post-launch numbers. The evidence here runs from research to decision, not from design to a measured result.

If I ran it again, a five-person test on the create-then-fill flow would come before the visual system. It was the one real disagreement with the brief, and the one thing I argued rather than proved.
